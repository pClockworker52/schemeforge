The big picture

What changed in the three days since the last overview, what we learned from it, and what the last Phase 3 round can still deliver.

Short answer

The swarm has now been tested and works in a modest way. Target A is split between two scheme families, and round 4 is set up to decide whether that split is a real law.

Three days ago the agents had never run. Since then they wrote the hunches, proposed the candidates and broke the leaders in round 3. The best candidate of the project came from a proposer agent: it passes 8 of the 10 Target A checks on held-out data. It fails steady-state smoothness, while the engine's best schemes are smooth but not sharp. Nobody has yet produced a scheme that is both.

Recommendation: run round 4 as planned, then stop Phase 3. Its outcome is useful either way. A scheme that is sharp and smooth would be a real result. Failing to find one, under a hunch registered beforehand, gives a clean negative result with evidence from four rounds.

The split

The two round-3 families sit on opposite sides of the target. The sharp one puts its shock into about 2 cells but makes the wall force jagged. The smooth one makes the wall force smooth but spreads its shock over 3.4 cells. The working explanation: a very sharp steady shock can only sit at a few sub-cell positions. As the back pressure moves the exact shock smoothly, the computed shock jumps from one position to the next, and the wall force jumps with it.

Held-out promo values, judge v1.4 (round-3 summary). Logarithmic roughness axis. Only candidates whose two numbers are in the round summaries are drawn; round 4 will add the zoo and every new promo-evaluated candidate, as the pre-registration requires.

3. What we learned

The judge keeps paying for itself

Every version closed a hole that would otherwise have produced a false winner. v1.1 caught candidates that guessed the case from the batch size. v1.4 caught the ratchet that froze late waves. v1.5 catches crashes and unit dependence. The breakers found 21 failure cases in one round; 4 were accepted. Held-out data again showed that dev results flatter.

The swarm helps, a little

Pre-registered swarm test: S1 met (agents produced ideas the main session had not), S2 met weakly (one of three engine islands improved on its seed, by ≤ 5 % on held-out data). The best idea came from a proposer. The engine polished, but crossover spread one mechanism to every island, and its scalar score led it the wrong way.

Target A may hold a real trade-off

For φ(r) limiters, sharp shocks and low overshoot don't go together (H-0011, supported). For reconstructions, sharp steady shocks and a smooth objective have not gone together either. That is now a pre-registered hunch, H-0019, with a kill criterion that round 4 tests directly.

  • Agents need hard rules for numbers. Two of six proposers skipped checking part of their own kill criterion, and both hunches turned out refuted. Every number must now cite a run id.
  • Positivity is the shared weakness. Nine crash cases across four leaders; van Leer survives all of them. Any promotable scheme will need a fallback.
  • Known methods keep reappearing. Round 3's mechanisms are rediscoveries or variants: characteristic THINC, BVD, limiter freezing. The search finds good combinations of known parts, not new parts.

5. Does continuing make sense?

For round 4, yes: it is cheap, fully specified, and every outcome ends Phase 3 with something to show. Beyond round 4 the plan does not continue Target A without a new recorded decision. The stronger long-term asset may already exist: an audited, cheat-resistant judge with held-out families and a breaker loop, which any scheme-search effort could use. Section 6 says how far that reaches.

How narrow it is. The method needs three things: a cheap, exact or provably reliable reference; a component small enough to be a pure function inside a fixed harness; and metrics that capture what practitioners care about. Exact solutions in milliseconds give the first. Where there is no ground truth (turbulence models judged against noisy experiments), where one evaluation takes hours, or where the value lies in a whole solver rather than one component, the approach does not fit. It is a tool for a family of problems, not a general method finder.

Does it save tokens? For a new arena, roughly the generic half of the code, all agent prompts and the process come for free. More importantly, the failure modes found here would not have to be rediscovered: candidates guessing the case from the batch size, absolute epsilons, overfitting to dev, memory thrashing, a scalar score that misleads the engine, duplicates in top-k picks. Each of those cost a judge version or a lost round here. The expensive part remains: exact references, their blind audit, meaningful metrics and held-out families have to be built for each new problem. My estimate: a new arena of similar size would take somewhat under half the effort Arena 1 did. That is a judgement from this project's history; tokens were not metered per phase.
