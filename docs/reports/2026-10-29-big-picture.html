The big picture

What the four search rounds on Arena 1 produced, why Target A was not reached, and what comes next.

Short answer

Phase 3 ended without a Target A promotion, but closer than expected. The last obstacle is Newton convergence on 3 of 20 held-out nozzles.

Round 4's best scheme, evolved by the engine, passes 12 of Target A's 13 checks on held-out data. It is sharp, it gives a smooth steady objective, and its overshoot is at van Leer's level. It fails only A4: Newton converges on 17 of 20 held-out nozzle instances, and A4 asks for all 20.

The explanation the plan bet on, that sharp steady shocks force a rough objective, did not hold up. Round 4 produced schemes that are both sharp and smooth, and a round-2 limiter already had been sharp, smooth and Newton-convergent at once. Target A's checks do conflict, but along several lines, not one.

Recommendation: finish the two open checks (H-0019 evidence, breakers), publish the negative-result note with the judge and zoo as a benchmark, and move to Phase 6. A round 5 aimed only at A4 for the nearest-miss family is the one continuation that looks worth a decision.

3. What we learned

The judge keeps paying for itself

Every version closed a hole that would otherwise have produced a false winner: case guessing (v1.1), memory that freezes late waves (v1.4), crashes and unit dependence (v1.5). Held-out data keeps exposing dev results: H-0025 had a roughness of 8.1e-6 on dev and 0.311 on held-out data.

Agents and engine both contribute

Round 3's best candidate came from a proposer agent, round 4's from the engine, after the screen and dedupe fixes. In both rounds crossover spread one mechanism to every island, so the islands say little about their own hunches. The swarm test was met for new ideas, and only weakly for improvement.

No single trade-off law

Two pre-registered "impossibility" hunches were tested. H-0011 (sharp φ(r) limiters overshoot) holds for the family swept. H-0019 (sharp steady shocks force a rough objective) is in doubt: its mechanism failed in round 4, and two round-2 limiters may still refute it formally.

  • Each mechanism found is a known one. Characteristic THINC, BVD, limiter freezing, motion-gated compression: good combinations of known parts, no new part.
  • Machines limit the pace more than models do. Memory pressure caused false timeouts three times (rounds 2 and 4); usage limits and an OS restart paused runs. The LLM cost of all of Arena 1 is $92.77 over 1,351 engine calls, plus agent sessions on the subscription.
  • The pre-registration had a factual error. Its mechanism paragraph said every round-2 near miss was rough or failed Newton; c-b5cb82a0ef was neither. The kill criterion itself was right, and the note now says so.

How narrow it is. The method needs three things: a cheap, exact or provably reliable reference; a component small enough to be a pure function inside a fixed harness; and metrics that capture what practitioners care about. Exact solutions in milliseconds give the first. Where there is no ground truth (turbulence models judged against noisy experiments), where one evaluation takes hours, or where the value lies in a whole solver rather than one component, the approach does not fit. It is a tool for a family of problems, not a general method finder.

Does it save tokens? For a new arena, roughly the generic half of the code, all agent prompts and the process come for free. More importantly, the failure modes found here would not have to be rediscovered: candidates guessing the case from the batch size, absolute epsilons, overfitting to dev, memory thrashing, a scalar score that misleads the engine, duplicates in top-k picks. Each of those cost a judge version or a lost round here. The expensive part remains: exact references, their blind audit, meaningful metrics and held-out families have to be built for each new problem. My estimate: a new arena of similar size would take somewhat under half the effort Arena 1 did. That is a judgement from this project's history; tokens were not metered per phase.
