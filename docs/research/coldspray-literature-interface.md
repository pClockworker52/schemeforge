﻿Literature Search: Mechanistic Models of Cold-Spray Bonding
1. Summary
Idea A (Bonding as local interface dissolution; Bay's cold-welding criterion; thickness-independent Y'; Markov blanket). Prior art contains the mechanical core of this idea partly in cold-welding and roll-bonding mechanics, and only tangentially in cold spray. The exact functional form for the bonded area fraction (Y - Y')/(1 - Y') combined with an extrusion pressure threshold p_E is the classical cold-pressure-welding model of Bay (1983, 1986), which was later embedded into a bonding-capable cohesive-zone finite-element interface by Kebriaei et al. (2014) for roll bonding. However, in Bay's formulation for brittle cover layers (Mechanism C, scratch-brushed films) vs. thin contaminant films (Mechanism F), the threshold expansion Y' for anodized or natural oxide films does vary empirically with film thickness and surface treatment in roll-bonding experiments, though for brittle work-hardened cover layers Bay treats the breakup onset as nearly zero or governed by layer ductility rather than thickness. In cold spray, qualitative and semi-quantitative "oxide breakup by interfacial stretch" arguments are widespread (e.g., Ichikawa & Ogawa 2015; Hassani-Gangaraj et al. 2018; Rahmati et al. 2021, who explicitly mesh an oxide shell with ductile damage and cohesive traction-separation in Abaqus), and contact-to-cohesive switching upon meeting a local threshold has been implemented using yield-stress drop (Pontabry et al. 2016) or contact establishment (Manap et al. 2011). No prior art in solid mechanics, tribology, or cold spray frames a material contact interface as a Pearl Markov blanket or formalizes interfacial state variables as a sufficient statistic screening off bulk fields.
Idea B (Heat conduction regularizes contact-edge localization in Lagrangian FEA). Prior art contains this idea fully in the dynamic-plasticity and adiabatic-shear-band (ASB) literature, and partly in cold-spray size-effect theory, but a direct comparative mesh-convergence study at the cold-spray contact edge isolating heat conduction vs. adiabatic Lagrangian failure is largely absent because cold-spray modelers historically blamed geometric element distortion rather than an ill-posed PDE. In computational mechanics, it is well established that rate-independent or weakly rate-dependent adiabatic thermal-softening plasticity loses ellipticity/hyperbolicity once thermal softening outweighs strain hardening, leading to zero-width localization and pathological mesh sensitivity (Needleman 1988; Batra & Kim 1991; Wright & Ockendon 1982; Grady & Kipp 1987). Adding thermal conductivity introduces the thermal diffusion length scale \delta_{\text{th}} \sim \sqrt{\alpha t} (or the Grady–Kipp/Batra–Kim coupled thermomechanical length scale), which sets a finite shear-band width and restores mesh convergence once element size h < \delta_{\text{th}}. In cold spray, Li et al. (2006) and Assadi et al. (2003, 2016) noted extreme mesh sensitivity of interfacial plastic strain and temperature in Lagrangian Abaqus/Explicit models and introduced the thermal diffusion length \sqrt{\alpha t} alongside particle size d_p to define an adiabaticity parameter (Schmidt et al. 2006), while Hassani-Gangaraj et al. (2018) contested the role of thermal diffusion and ASB in setting v_{\text{cr}}.
Idea C (Explosive-welding collision-point window applied to sphere impact predicts a bonded ring). Prior art contains this idea conceptually and experimentally, though rarely derived as a formal closed-form mapping of the 2D (\beta, V_c) Carpenter–Deribas–Walsh window onto spherical coordinates. The kinematic analogy between the sweeping contact periphery of an impacting cold-spray sphere and the oblique collision point in explosive welding—where the collision angle \beta(r) increases from 0^\circ at the south pole while the radial contact-point sweep velocity V_c(r) = v_p \cot\beta drops from \infty at initial contact through the shock/bulk sound speed C_b—was explicitly formulated in the shock-wave hydrodynamic plasticity model of Hassani-Gangaraj et al. (2018) (building on Walsh et al. 1953 jetless vs. jetting oblique impact regimes) and earlier explosive-welding analogies (Grujicic et al. 2003; Assadi et al. 2003). Furthermore, the prediction and observation that the central "south pole" remains unbonded while metallurgical bonding forms an annular ring around it is well documented in single-particle cold-spray literature (e.g., Bae et al. 2008; King et al. 2010; Panova & Schuh 2024; Tang, Ichikawa & Hassani 2025).
Idea D (Rebound and restitution deficit below v_{\text{cr}} as a measure of interface work). Prior art contains the energy-balance premise of this idea, particularly in models that pit elastic rebound strain energy against interfacial adhesion work (Wu et al. 2006; Kuroda et al. 2008) and in LIPIT coefficient-of-restitution (e = v_r / v_i) analyses that deviate from standard Johnson (e \propto v_i^{-1/4}) elasto-plastic impact scaling near v_{\text{cr}}. Specifically, Tiamiyu et al. (2022) directly analyzed LIPIT coefficient-of-restitution curves below v_{\text{cr}} and identified an anomalous energy dissipation regime prior to bonding, attributing the extra kinetic energy loss to interfacial work—specifically the fracture and delamination of the native oxide shell—rather than bulk plastic dissipation alone. More recently, Tang, Ichikawa & Hassani (2025) combined sub-v_{\text{cr}} LIPIT restitution data with finite-element elastic-recovery energy calculations and micro-tensile testing to quantify how interfacial bond energy arrests rebound at v_{\text{cr}} and governs elastic-recovery damage above v_{\text{cr}}.
2. Detailed Findings (Q1 to Q5)
Q1: Idea A — Interface Dissolution, Bay's Criterion, Contact Switching, and Markov Blankets
Q1.1 Bay's Theory of Cold Pressure Welding
* Bay, N. (1979). "Cold Pressure Welding—The Mechanisms Governing Bonding." Journal of Engineering for Industry (Transactions of the ASME), 101(2), 121–127. DOI: 10.1115/1.3439484 [S — checked via citations and mathematical summaries in Bay 1983 and Kebriaei et al. 2014].
* Bay, N. (1983). "Mechanisms Producing Metallic Bonds in Cold Welding." Welding Journal (Research Supplement), 62(5), 137s–142s. Link: [https://files.aws.org/wj/supplement/WJ_1983_05_s137.pdf](https://files.aws.org/wj/supplement/WJ_1983_05_s137.pdf) [F/S — checked extended text excerpts and equations via indexed text].
* Bay, N. (1986). "Cold Welding: Part 1—Characteristics, Bond Strength, Bond Mechanism." Metal Construction, 18(6), 369–372. [S — checked via citations in Danesh Manesh & Karimi Taheri 2009 / Sargazi et al. 2018].
Exact Formulas in Bay (1983): Bay distinguishes two mechanisms on surfaces prepared for cold welding:
1. Mechanism C (Cover Layer): Applies to the fraction of the nominal surface area \beta (often written \psi in later papers) covered by a brittle, work-hardened surface layer (e.g., produced by scratch-brushing). Because this brittle layer has negligible ductility, it fractures at the very beginning of plastic surface expansion (Y \approx 0), and blocks of the cover layer remain constant in area while virgin base metal extrudes between them once the normal pressure p exceeds an extrusion pressure p_E.
2. Mechanism F (Contaminant Film): Applies to the remaining fraction (1 - \beta) of the surface covered by thin contaminant/oxide films (or anodized/chemical films). This film does not fracture until the surface expansion Y = (A - A_0)/A (where A_0 is initial area and A is deformed area, so 1 - Y = A_0/A) reaches a threshold surface expansion Y'. Once Y > Y', the film breaks up and the true area fraction of exposed virgin metal within that region is given geometrically by: \frac{A - A'}{A} = \frac{Y - Y'}{1 - Y'} where A' is the area at the onset of film fracture (Y' = (A' - A_0)/A').
Combining Mechanisms C and F via force equilibrium, Bay's equation (Eq. 6 in Bay 1983) for the nominal bond tensile strength \sigma_B normalized by the uniaxial yield/flow stress \sigma_0 is:
\frac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_0} = (1 - \beta)\, Y \, \frac{p - p_E}{\sigma_0} + \beta \, \frac{Y - Y'}{1 - Y'} \, \frac{p}{\sigma_0}
(Note on notation: in Bay 1983, \beta is the area fraction governed by the threshold film mechanism F and 1-\beta is the scratch-brushed cover-layer fraction governed by extrusion pressure p_E; in several later papers such as Kebriaei et al. 2014, the weights \alpha and 1-\alpha are swapped so that \alpha weights the (Y-Y')/(1-Y')p term and 1-\alpha weights the Y\langle p - p_E \rangle term. When no scratch-brushed cover layer is present, only the oxide/contaminant film mechanism acts, or when extrusion through cracks in the fractured oxide is required, authors combine (Y - Y')/(1 - Y') with (p - p_E)).
* Threshold conditions: In Bay (1983) Eq. 6, if p < p_E, the first term is set to zero (p = p_E); if Y < Y', the second term is set to zero (Y = Y'). Furthermore, the local true bond strength cannot exceed the flow stress, imposing an upper cutoff where the cracks coalesce and plastic extrusion saturates.
* Extrusion pressure p_E: Derived by Bay using upper-bound / slip-line-field plane-strain extrusion of virgin metal into channel cracks of width w and oxide/cover-layer thickness t. When the crack opening is narrow compared to the cover layer thickness, p_E / \sigma_0 depends on the aspect ratio of the channel (and drops as surface expansion Y widens the gap between oxide islands); for typical scratch-brushed Al-Al, Bay uses a constant effective threshold p_E / \sigma_0 \approx 1.85 to 2.0 (on the order of twice the uniaxial flow stress \sigma_0).
* Does Y' depend on cover-layer thickness or only on type/preparation? In Bay (1983), for Al-Al cold welding, Bay writes that welding under Mechanism F requires reaching "a threshold surface expansion Y' necessary to break the contaminant film" (citing Y' = 0.35 for the non-brushed contaminant film on Al in his Fig. 3/Eq. 6 fit). In the broader cold-roll-bonding literature following Bay (e.g., Madaah-Hosseini & Kokabi 2002; Zhang & Bay 1997), Y' is treated primarily as a property of the surface preparation and oxide/contaminant type (e.g., degreased vs. anodized vs. vacuum-baked), because a continuous brittle oxide film coherent with the substrate fractures when the substrate interfacial stretch reaches the fracture strain of that specific oxide structure. However, if the extrusion pressure p_E through the cracked oxide is retained as a function of channel geometry, p_E increases with oxide thickness t_{\text{ox}} because deeper oxide channels require higher pressure to extrude fresh metal to the contact plane.
Q1.2 Surface-Expansion or Oxide-Breakup Criteria in Cold Spray
* Rahmati, S., Veiga, R. G. A., Zúñiga, A., & Jodoin, B. (2021). "A Numerical Approach to Study the Oxide Layer Effect on Adhesion in Cold Spray." Journal of Thermal Spray Technology, 30(7), 1777–1791. DOI: 10.1007/s11666-021-01245-4 [S/A — checked via extended snippets and citing descriptions].
   * Criterion & Parameters: Instead of an analytical Bay formula, Rahmati et al. explicitly model the thin native oxide shell on the particle and substrate in Abaqus/Explicit alongside a surface-based cohesive behavior between the bare metal surfaces. Oxide breakup is governed by a ductile damage initiation criterion (plastic strain at damage initiation) and a critical plastic displacement at failure u_f^{\text{pl}} (e.g., u_f^{\text{pl}} = 0.00025\text{ }\mu\text{m}). Once the oxide elements fail and delete due to interfacial stretching, the underlying fresh metal surfaces make contact and bond if they satisfy the cohesive traction-separation criterion.
   * Validation: Validated against single-particle critical velocity trends and bonded interface locations (predicting bonded rings vs. unbonded regions) across varying oxide thicknesses for Cu.
* MacDonald, D., et al. (2025). "Numerical Examination of Particle and Substrate Oxide Layer Failure and Porosity Formation in Coatings Deposited Using Liquid Cold Spray." Coatings, 15(4), 405. DOI: 10.3390/coatings15040405 [S — checked via snippet].
   * Criterion: Adopts the explicit oxide-shell damage/breakup methodology of Rahmati et al. (2021) to track oxide fracture and fresh-metal exposure.
* Ichikawa, Y., & Ogawa, K. (2015). "Effect of Substrate Surface Oxide Film Thickness on Deposition Behavior and Deposition Efficiency in the Cold Spray Process." Journal of Thermal Spray Technology, 24(7), 1269–1276. DOI: 10.1007/s11666-015-0299-y [S/A — checked via ResearchGate full-text preview snippets].
   * Criterion: Qualitative/experimental newly-formed surface (surface expansion) argument: thin oxide films do not alter bulk particle plastic flattening, so the drop in deposition efficiency with thicker oxide is governed by whether interfacial area expansion is sufficient to crack the oxide and extrude virgin metal through the gaps.
* Fukuda, Y., Cote, D., Nault, I. M., Nardi, A., Champagne, V. K., Ma, Y., & Palko, J. W. (2021). "Particle-based simulation of cold spray: Influence of oxide layer on impact process." Additive Manufacturing, 37, 101517. DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2020.101517 [S — checked via metadata and citing snippets].
   * Criterion: Uses a meshfree particle-based method (SPH/peridynamics-like) with an explicit brittle oxide shell to simulate oxide breakup and fresh metal contact as a function of interfacial stretch and oxide thickness.
Q1.3 Simulations Switching from Sliding Contact to Tied/Cohesive Upon a Criterion
* Kebriaei, R., Frischkorn, J., Reese, S., Husmann, T., & Kreimeyer, M. (2014). "Numerical modelling of powder metallurgical coatings on ring-shaped parts integrated with ring rolling." Journal of Materials Processing Technology, 214(10), 2146–2155. DOI: 10.1016/j.jmatprotec.2014.03.014 and Kebriaei, R., & Vladimirov, I. N., & Reese, S. (2014). "Joining by plastic deformation: modeling and finite element implementation." (Also expanded in ResearchGate preprint: "Modeling of joining by plastic deformation using a bonding interface finite element"). [F — checked full text of the interface formulation via ResearchGate].
   * Solver & Criterion: Custom user interface element (FEAP / Abaqus UEL). Before contact and during compression, the interface acts as penalty contact with zero tensile bond strength (\sigma_B = 0). As normal pressure p and tangential surface expansion \psi = (A - A_0)/A evolve during compression, the local cohesive strength \sigma_B evolves dynamically according to Bay's equation: \sigma_B = \alpha \left\langle \frac{\psi - \psi_0}{1 - \psi_0} \right\rangle p + (1 - \alpha) \psi \langle p - p_{\text{ex}} \rangle When the surfaces subsequently experience tensile/shear separation, a bilinear cohesive zone law with peak traction equal to the accumulated \sigma_B resists debonding.
   * Verification: Verified on benchmark single-element compression-tension cycles and validated against cold roll-bonding experiments. (Not applied to supersonic cold spray in their paper, but it is the exact finite-element realization of Idea A).
* Pontabry, N., (et al.) (2016). "Numerical analysis of single particle impact in the context of Cold Spray: a new adhesion model." IOP Conference Series: Materials Science and Engineering (or Earth and Environmental Science), ResearchGate ID 301509408. [F — checked via ResearchGate PDF text].
   * Solver & Criterion: SPH fast-dynamics solver (hexagonal compact lattice, particle radius 1.25\text{ }\mu\text{m} for 25\text{ }\mu\text{m} Cu impact). Contacting surface elements slide/compress until a local physical activation criterion is met on both contacting elements: a 30% drop in the local Johnson–Cook yield stress (representing thermal softening / shear localization) while damage D < D_{\text{max}}. Once activated, separating elements switch to an energy-dissipative cohesive zone model governed by a surface adhesion energy parameter; the link breaks if all adhesion energy is dissipated during rebound or if bulk damage exceeds D_{\text{max}}.
   * Verification: Verified by tracking the number of contacting pairs, activated adhesive pairs, pairs broken by rebound energy dissipation, and pairs broken by high-speed erosion damage, successfully reproducing both the lower critical velocity v_{\text{cr}} and the upper erosion velocity limit.
* Manap, A., Nooririnah, O., Misran, H., & Sahab, A. R. M. (2011). "Computer simulation of cold sprayed deposition using smoothed particle hydrodynamics." Procedia Engineering, 10, 1145–1150. DOI: 10.1016/j.proeng.2011.04.190 [F — checked full text via snippet].
   * Solver & Criterion: SPH solver with a Dugdale–Barenblatt cohesive zone model (constant traction \sigma_0 for separation \delta < \delta_c). The cohesive traction is switched on automatically when SPH particles of the two bodies come within 2h (twice the smoothing length) and form mutually conforming contact surfaces.
   * Verification: Verified by comparing predicted rebound distance vs. impact velocity for Al-on-Al against Eulerian simulations and experimental deposition windows.
* Rahmati, S., Veiga, R. G. A., Zúñiga, A., & Jodoin, B. (2021). Journal of Thermal Spray Technology, 30(7), 1777–1791. DOI: 10.1007/s11666-021-01245-4 [S/A].
   * Solver & Criterion: Abaqus/Explicit. Uses surface-based cohesive behavior / tied contact that activates only when the intervening meshed oxide layer elements reach their ductile damage failure displacement u_f^{\text{pl}} and are deleted, allowing the underlying virgin metal surfaces to touch.
Q1.4 Material Interface as a Markov Blanket
No prior art found in mechanics or cold spray. While the term "Markov blanket" is occasionally used in active-inference/complex-systems preprints to describe generic physical boundaries or phase interfaces (e.g., biehl / Kauffman or Preprints.org 202306.1322 [S]), no paper in contact mechanics, solid-state welding, or cold spray formulates the contact interface as a Pearl Markov blanket (conditional independence screening off bulk continuum fields given local interfacial state variables \{p, Y, \Delta u_t, T, \text{oxide state}\}).
Q2: Idea B — Heat Conduction Regularization of Contact-Edge Localization
Q2.1 Thermal-Conduction and Viscous Regularization of Adiabatic Shear Bands in FEA
* Needleman, A. (1988). "Material rate dependence and mesh sensitivity in localization problems." Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering, 67(1), 69–85. DOI: 10.1016/0045-7825(88)90069-2 [A/S — checked via abstract and literature citations].
   * Mechanism & Convergence: Shows that for rate-independent strain-softening or thermally softening solids, the governing incremental boundary-value problem loses ellipticity (or hyperbolicity in dynamics) at the onset of localization; the shear band width shrinks to a single element row and plastic strain diverges as h \to 0. Rate dependence (viscoplasticity) introduces an implicit time/length scale through wave propagation (L_{\text{visc}} \sim c \tau or \sigma_y / (\rho \dot{\gamma}^2)) that regularizes the PDE.
* Batra, R. C., & Kim, C. H. (1991). "Effect of thermal conductivity on the initiation, growth and bandwidth of adiabatic shear bands." International Journal of Engineering Science, 29(8), 949–960. DOI: 10.1016/0020-7225(91)90068-E [A/S — checked via Batra's subsequent open-access papers and citations].
   * Length Scale & Convergence: Demonstrates in finite-element simulations of viscoplastic thermomechanical simple shear that when thermal conductivity k = 0 (strictly adiabatic), the localized plastic strain depends severely on the mesh. Including k > 0 introduces diffusive heat flux k \nabla^2 T into the energy equation, which penalizes high-wavenumber temperature perturbations (acting analogously to strain hardening at short wavelengths, scaling as k \xi^2 where \xi is wavenumber) and sets a finite shear-band width on the order of the thermal diffusion length \delta_{\text{th}} \sim \sqrt{\alpha t}. Mesh convergence of peak strain and temperature is achieved once the finite elements inside the band are smaller than \delta_{\text{th}}.
* Grady, D. E., & Kipp, M. E. (1987). "The growth of unstable thermoplastic shear with application to steady-wave shock compression in solids." Journal of the Mechanics and Physics of Solids, 35(1), 95–119. DOI: 10.1016/0022-5096(87)90030-5 [S — checked via exact equation citations in J. Appl. Phys. 2025, DOI: 10.1063/5.0250615 and LANL Report LA-12667-MS].
   * Length Scale Formulas: Grady and Kipp balance thermal diffusion against momentum diffusion during catastrophic thermal softening, deriving the optimal localization time \tau_{\text{GK}} and the mature shear-band width \delta_{\text{GK}}: \tau_{\text{GK}} = \left[ \frac{36 \rho^3 C_p^2 \alpha}{a^2 \sigma_{y0} \dot{\gamma}^3} \right]^{1/4}, \qquad \delta_{\text{GK}} = \sqrt{2 \alpha \tau_{\text{GK}}} = \left[ \frac{144 \rho C_p^2 k^3}{a^2 \sigma_{y0} \dot{\gamma}^3} \right]^{1/8} where \alpha = k / (\rho C_p) is thermal diffusivity, a = -(\partial \sigma_y / \partial T)/\sigma_{y0} is the thermal softening coefficient, and \dot{\gamma} is the nominal shear strain rate.
* Batra, R. C., & Chen, L. (2001). "Instability analysis and shear band spacing in gradient-dependent thermoviscoplastic materials." Archives of Mechanics, 53(2), 167–192. Link: [https://batra.me.vt.edu/content/dam/batra_me_vt_edu/publications/archives2001(167-192).pdf](https://batra.me.vt.edu/content/dam/batra_me_vt_edu/publications/archives2001(167-192).pdf) [F — checked full PDF].
   * Length Scale: Reviews the Bai (1982), Wright & Ockendon (1982), Molinari (1997), and Batra linear perturbation dispersion relations showing that thermal conductivity k provides a cutoff wavenumber \xi_m, whereas for k \to 0 without strain-rate gradients, the fastest-growing wavenumber \xi_m \to \infty (zero band width).
Q2.2 Cold-Spray Impact Simulations With Heat Conduction and Mesh Convergence
* Assadi, H., Gärtner, F., Stoltenhoff, T., & Kreye, H. (2003). "Bonding mechanism in cold gas spraying." Acta Materialia, 51(15), 4379–4394. DOI: 10.1016/S1359-6454(03)00274-X [F/S — checked via ResearchGate excerpt and Assadi et al. 2016 review].
   * Findings: Performed coupled temperature-displacement Lagrangian FEA in Abaqus/Explicit (both adiabatic and with heat conduction). They observed that plastic strain and temperature at the contact periphery (the jetting edge) did not converge with mesh refinement in adiabatic runs, and explicitly noted that at sub-micron element sizes and nanosecond timescales, heat conduction reduces the peak interfacial temperature compared to strictly adiabatic assumptions.
* Li, W.-Y., Liao, H., Li, C.-J., Li, G., Coddet, C., & Wang, X. (2006). "On high velocity impact of micro-sized metallic particles in cold spraying." Applied Surface Science, 253(5), 2852–2862. DOI: 10.1016/j.apsusc.2006.05.126 [S — checked via citations in Chalmers thesis 522167 and Assadi et al. 2016].
   * Findings: Investigated mesh-size sensitivity (down to 1/100\text{ }d_p, i.e., 0.2\text{ }\mu\text{m} for a 20\text{ }\mu\text{m} particle) in Abaqus/Explicit Lagrangian and ALE simulations, comparing adiabatic vs. coupled thermal-mechanical runs. Found that while macroscopic flattening ratio converges at moderate mesh sizes, the localized edge plastic strain and jetting lip in Lagrangian simulations remain strongly mesh-dependent and prematurely terminate due to element distortion; coupled heat conduction lowers the peak interfacial temperature and delays thermal softening compared to adiabatic runs, though Lagrangian distortion still occurs if severe jetting develops.
* Hassani-Gangaraj, M., Veysset, D., Champagne, V. K., Nelson, K. A., & Schuh, C. A. (2018). "Adiabatic shear instability is not necessary for adhesion in cold spray." Acta Materialia, 158, 430–439. DOI: 10.1016/j.actamat.2018.07.065 [F — checked via ResearchGate / Researcher.Life full text].
   * Findings: Used coupled Eulerian–Lagrangian (CEL) simulations in Abaqus/Explicit (mesh size d_p/100 to d_p/250) with heat conduction included. Rather than comparing conduction vs. no-conduction for mesh convergence, they compared simulations with and without the Johnson–Cook thermal softening term ([1 - T^{*m}]). They showed that jetting at the contact edge still initiates even when thermal softening is completely turned off, proving that the peripheral lip is driven by pressure-wave interaction with the free surface (hydrodynamic plasticity) rather than a purely thermal-softening instability. However, they noted that "the jet region is slightly more extended in the former case where thermal softening contributes to the deformation".
Q2.3 Thermal-Diffusion Explanation of the Size Dependence of v_{\text{cr}}
* Schmidt, T., Gärtner, F., Assadi, H., & Kreye, H. (2006). "Development of a generalized parameter window for cold spray deposition." Acta Materialia, 54(3), 729–742. DOI: 10.1016/j.actamat.2005.10.005 [A/S — checked via abstract and Assadi et al. 2016 full text].
* Assadi, H., Kreye, H., Gärtner, F., & Klassen, T. (2016). "Cold spraying – A materials perspective." Acta Materialia, 116, 382–407. DOI: 10.1016/j.actamat.2016.06.034 [F — checked full text via CORE PDF].
   * The Thermal-Diffusion Mechanism: Schmidt et al. (2006) and Assadi et al. (2016) argue that bonding requires adiabatic shear instability (ASI), which in turn requires the shear zone to remain nearly adiabatic during the impact time t_{\text{imp}} \sim d_p / v_p. They compare the characteristic system dimension x (proportional to particle diameter d_p) to the thermal diffusion distance \delta_{\text{th}} \sim \sqrt{\alpha t} via a dimensionless adiabaticity parameter (Fourier/Péclet-type ratio, Eq. 2a in Assadi et al. 2016): \beta_{\text{ad}} \sim \frac{x}{\sqrt{\alpha t}} \propto \sqrt{\frac{d_p v_p}{\alpha}} As particle diameter d_p decreases, the surface-to-volume ratio of the shear zone increases and the cooling rate by thermal conduction (\propto \alpha / d_p^2) quenches the interfacial temperature rise. Consequently, smaller particles require a higher impact velocity v_{\text{cr}} to generate heat faster than thermal diffusion removes it.
* Hassani-Gangaraj, M., Veysset, D., Champagne, V. K., Nelson, K. A., & Schuh, C. A. (2018 / 2019). Acta Materialia, 158, 430–439 (10.1016/j.actamat.2018.07.065) and Response to Comment, Scripta Materialia (2019) [F/S]:
   * Critique & Tests of the Mechanism: Hassani-Gangaraj et al. argue that while thermal diffusion contributes to cooling at small scales, ASI is not necessary for jetting; in subsequent LIPIT works by the MIT group (including excluded papers B14 Lienhard 2020 and B05 Chen 2022, plus Dowding et al.), the d_p-dependence of v_{\text{cr}} is tested against competing explanations:
      1. Oxide-to-volume ratio (t_{\text{ox}}/d_p): A native oxide shell of fixed thickness t_{\text{ox}} represents a larger volume fraction of a smaller particle (t_{\text{ox}}/d_p), requiring more kinetic energy per unit particle mass to fracture and delaminate (tested by varying oxide thickness independently of particle size; see Na et al. 2025 in Q5 and Tiamiyu et al. 2022 in Q4).
      2. Strain-rate hardening and dislocation mechanics: Smaller particles experience higher characteristic strain rates \dot{\varepsilon} \sim v_p / d_p, increasing dynamic flow stress.
Q3: Idea C — Explosive-Welding Windows Applied to Sphere Impact
Q3.1 Classical Explosive-Welding Window Formulas
* Walsh, J. M., Shreffler, R. G., & Willig, F. J. (1953). "Limiting conditions for jet formation in high velocity collisions." Journal of Applied Physics, 24(3), 349–359. DOI: 10.1063/1.1721278 [S — checked via exact citations in Hassani-Gangaraj et al. 2018 and MDPI Metals 2019].
* Cowan, G. R., Bergmann, O. R., & Holtzman, A. H. (1971). "Mechanism of bond zone wave formation in explosion-clad metals." Metallurgical Transactions, 2(11), 3145–3155. DOI: 10.1007/BF02814967 [S — checked via MDPI Metals 2019].
* Deribas, A. A., Simonov, V. A., & Zakcharenko, I. D. (1975). "Investigation of explosive welding parameters for arbitrary combinations of metals and alloys." Proceedings of the 5th International Conference on High Energy Rate Fabrication, Denver, CO. [S — checked via exact equations in Shi et al., Metals 2019, 9(3), 339, DOI: 10.3390/met9030339].
* Wittman, R. H. (1973) / Carpenter, S. H., & Wittman, R. H. (1975). "Explosion welding." Annual Review of Materials Science, 5(1), 177–199. DOI: 10.1146/annurev.ms.05.080175.001141 [S — checked via Shi et al. 2019, DOI: 10.3390/met9030339].
The Four Boundaries of the (\beta, V_c) Weldability Window (as compiled in Shi et al. 2019, DOI: 10.3390/met9030339 [F]): Let V_c be the collision-point sweep velocity along the interface, \beta the dynamic collision angle, and V_p the normal flyer impact velocity (for parallel plate impact, V_p = 2 V_c \sin(\beta/2) \approx V_c \beta).
1. Lower collision-angle limit \beta_{\min} for jetting / plastic flow (Deribas 1975): \beta = k_1 \sqrt{\frac{H_V}{\rho V_c^2}} = \frac{k_1}{V_c} \left( \frac{H_V}{\rho} \right)^{1/2} where H_V is the Vickers hardness (in Pa), \rho is the flyer density, and k_1 \in [0.6, 1.2] is a surface-cleanliness constant (k_1 = 0.6 for clean, well-prepared surfaces; 1.2 for oxidized/uncleaned surfaces). (Notice that since V_p \approx \beta V_c, Deribas's lower boundary immediately reduces to a constant normal velocity threshold V_p \ge k_1 \sqrt{H_V / \rho}!). An alternative lower boundary by Belyaev / Lysak & Kuzmin includes a compressibility correction near the sound speed: \beta = 1.8 \left[ \frac{1}{V_c}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_b}{\rho}} + 0.1 \left( \frac{V_c}{C_b + 1550} \right)^2 \right]
2. Maximum collision-point velocity limit V_{c,\max} for shock attachment / jet release (Walsh et al. 1953): For a supersonic collision point (V_c > C_b, where C_b = \sqrt{B/\rho} is the bulk sound speed), an attached shock wave prevents a forward-shooting surface jet unless the turning angle \beta is below a critical shock-detachment angle \beta_c(V_c). In standard explosive-welding windows, this sets the right-hand boundary: V_c < C_b \quad (\text{or } V_c < 1.25\, C_b \text{ with } \beta > \beta_{\text{detachment}}(V_c/C_b))
3. Transition from smooth/linear to wavy interface V_{c,\text{trans}} (Cowan et al. 1971): governed by a critical fluid-like Reynolds number at the stagnation point: R_e = \frac{(\rho_f + \rho_p) V_c^2}{2 (H_f + H_p)} \approx 8 \text{ to } 10
4. Upper collision-angle / velocity limit for interfacial melting (Wittman 1973): \sin\frac{\beta}{2} = 14.7 \, V_c^{-5/4} \left( \frac{T_m k}{\alpha^{1/2} \rho \, t_f^{1/2}} \right) \left( \frac{t_p}{t_f + t_p} \right)
Q3.2 Application to Sphere Impact in Cold Spray and Prediction of a Bonded Ring
* Hassani-Gangaraj, M., Veysset, D., Champagne, V. K., Nelson, K. A., & Schuh, C. A. (2018). Acta Materialia, 158, 430–439. DOI: 10.1016/j.actamat.2018.07.065 [F].
   * Kinematics Applied to Sphere Impact: In Section 3 ("What causes jetting if not adiabatic shear instability?", Fig. 4), Hassani-Gangaraj et al. explicitly apply Walsh et al. (1953) oblique-shock collision-point kinematics to a spherical microparticle of radius R impacting a flat substrate at velocity v_p. At initial touch at the south pole (r = 0), the contact angle is \beta = 0 and the geometric contact point expands radially faster than the shock wave speed (V_c > C_{\text{shock}}), so the shock wave is attached to the contact periphery, keeping the south pole under hydrostatic compression without a free-surface release jet. As the contact radius r(t) sweeps outward, \sin\beta = r/R increases and the contact-edge sweep speed drops. At a critical angle/radius where the shock wave detaches from the contact periphery and meets the free surface, a strong tensile release wave triggers outward hydrodynamic jetting.
